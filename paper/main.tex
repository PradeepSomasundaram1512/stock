\title{Does the LLM Add Alpha? A Contamination-Free Forward Audit of a Deployed Multi-Agent LLM Trading System}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Pradeep Somasundaram}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Independent Researcher\\
aadhi1501@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent large language model (LLM) trading systems are usually evaluated with backtests that overlap the model's training window and credit the LLM for returns earned by the surrounding rules. We audit a deployed seven-node LangGraph agent (GPT-4o-mini, called by alias) on U.S. large- and mid-cap stocks, using \nRaw{} decisions logged in real time between January and September 2026, all after the model's knowledge cutoff. Our Forward Audit Protocol, assembled from standard tools and validated by Monte Carlo on the real price panel, compares the LLM's picks with characteristic-matched placebos and adds calibration, specification--deployment and operational audits. At the native intraday horizon the LLM's abnormal return over its placebo was \pct{\iAlpha} per trade (95\% CI \pct{\iAlphaCI}), ruling out any edge above \pct{\mdeUpperIntra}; that placebo is unbiased and the inference is well calibrated (false-positive rate \ipSizeBoot\% against a nominal 5\%). Over ten days, \nBuy{} BUY signals earned \pct{\mBuyRawten}, fully explained by market exposure: the alpha over the placebo was \pct{\mBuyMinusMatch} (CI \pct{\mBuyMinusMatchCI}), and \pct{\hSixEst} (CI \hSixCI\%) after removing the ten-day placebo's \powBias{} pp bias for oversold picks. The operational audit found that \opFallbackPct\% of logged decisions were silent exception fallbacks from an exhausted API quota. This is a one-model case study with about five usable months; a pre-registered forward test is running.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLM-based trading agents have progressed from sentiment classifiers~\cite{lopezlira2023chatgpt,yang2023fingpt} to tool-using multi-agent systems that read news, fundamentals and price charts before issuing orders~\cite{yu2023finmem,zhang2024finagent,xiao2024tradingagents,ding2024survey}. The reported gains, however, rest on two fragile foundations. First, backtests over historical periods overlap the LLM's pre-training corpus, so the model may ``remember'' outcomes~\cite{glasserman2023lookahead}. Second, deployed agents are \emph{pipelines}: a technical pre-screen chooses what the LLM sees, deterministic gates edit its output, and hand-coded rules set exits. A positive P\&L therefore says little about whether the LLM itself adds information.

This paper asks a narrower but more useful question: \emph{given everything else in the pipeline, does the LLM's choice add return?} We answer it with a forward, contamination-free audit of a system that ran in production for nine months. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A deployed multi-agent LLM trading system} with a four-stage funnel (pre-screen $\rightarrow$ seven-node LangGraph agent $\rightarrow$ deterministic gates $\rightarrow$ ATR exits), and a real-time log of \nRaw{} decisions on \nLogTick{} tickers made after the model's knowledge cutoff.
\item \textbf{The Forward Audit Protocol (FAP)}, an audit assembled from standard tools (a characteristic-matched placebo and block-bootstrap inference) plus a specification--deployment consistency audit with a counterfactual exit analysis, a confidence-calibration audit and an operational audit of the production logs, \emph{validated by Monte Carlo} on the real price panel for size, power and placebo bias (Section~\ref{sec:valid}).
\item \textbf{Empirical evidence} that (a) the agent's selection alpha is statistically indistinguishable from zero at every horizon from intraday to ten days, with the detectable effect size stated for each; (b) the confidence recorded by the pipeline (after its gates) was over-confident and carried no rank information, most clearly at the native intraday horizon; (c) replaying the deployed intraday stop levels on the intended ten-day hold shows they would have discarded the market drift the selections captured; and (d) \opFallbackPct\% of the logged decisions were fallbacks from an exhausted API quota, a failure that became visible only when the logs were audited.
\end{enumerate}
The negative result is the point: an agent whose signals earned positive raw returns in a rising market can have no measurable LLM contribution once the right counterfactual is used, and a system that appears to run continuously can spend most of its decisions on a fallback path.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{LLMs in finance.} Domain LLMs such as BloombergGPT~\cite{wu2023bloomberggpt} and FinGPT~\cite{yang2023fingpt} showed that language models can extract financial signals; Lopez-Lira and Tang~\cite{lopezlira2023chatgpt} reported that ChatGPT headline scores predict next-day returns. \textbf{LLM trading agents} add memory (FinMem~\cite{yu2023finmem}), multimodal tools (FinAgent~\cite{zhang2024finagent}) and role-playing analyst teams (TradingAgents~\cite{xiao2024tradingagents}); see~\cite{ding2024survey} for a survey. Most early work evaluates on historical windows that pre-date or overlap model training. Recent benchmarks close that gap by evaluating agents live or on post-cutoff windows: StockBench~\cite{chen2025stockbench} finds that most LLM agents struggle to beat buy-and-hold, AI-Trader~\cite{fan2025aitrader} reports that general intelligence does not ensure trading success, and Agent Market Arena~\cite{qian2025agents} finds that agent architecture shapes behavior more than the underlying model. These studies compare agents with each other or with passive baselines; they do not isolate what the LLM's choice adds inside one deployed pipeline, which is the question we ask.

\textbf{Evaluation pitfalls.} Glasserman and Lin~\cite{glasserman2023lookahead} documented look-ahead bias in GPT-based return prediction, Lopez-Lira et al.~\cite{lopezlira2025memorization} showed that LLMs recall economic and financial data verbatim from before their cutoff and that instructions to respect historical boundaries do not stop it, and Gao et al.~\cite{gao2025lookahead} give a statistical test for it that finds the bias disappears after the cutoff, which motivates forward evaluation. Kong et al.~\cite{kong2026bias} review 164 papers and catalogue five recurring biases (look-ahead, survivorship, narrative, objective and cost) in financial LLM work, and Ye et al.~\cite{ye2026alphaillusion} argue that reported LLM-agent alpha should not be treated as deployment evidence and propose a minimum reporting protocol. FAP is narrower and complementary: it supplies a placebo-based attribution, stated power, and an operational audit for a single deployed system. Data-snooping and multiple testing inflate backtest Sharpe ratios~\cite{harvey2016cross,bailey2014deflated}. Finance has long used characteristic-matched benchmarks~\cite{daniel1997measuring} and event studies~\cite{brown1985event} to isolate skill from exposure; we bring these tools to LLM agents and add dependence-robust inference~\cite{kunsch1989jackknife,newey1987simple}.

\textbf{LLM confidence.} Neural networks are often mis-calibrated~\cite{guo2017calibration}; LLMs partly know what they know~\cite{kadavath2022know}, but verbalized confidence tends to be over-confident~\cite{xiong2024uncertainty}. Trading agents nonetheless use it as a position gate, which we test directly.

\textbf{Stage 1} computes hourly RSI-14~\cite{wilder1978new}, a volume ratio and short-term momentum for every constituent and forwards at most 50 candidates. \textbf{Stage 2} is a LangGraph~\cite{langgraph} directed graph of seven nodes: price retrieval, technical features (RSI, MACD, ATR, trend, support/resistance), fundamentals (valuation, health and growth scores, analyst targets), news sentiment, market context (VIX, index and sector returns), retrieval-augmented context from a vector store of market patterns and risk guidelines~\cite{lewis2020rag}, and a reasoning node. The reasoning node prompts GPT-4o-mini~\cite{openai2024gpt4o} (knowledge cutoff October 2023) with the technical, fundamental, news and market-context outputs (the retrieved context is computed but never inserted into the prompt; Appendix~\ref{sec:abl}) and returns a JSON object with decision, confidence, target, stop and rationale. Deep analysis of the selected candidates ran in parallel worker threads. If the model call raises an exception, the agent returns HOLD with confidence 0.5 and logs the error; Section~\ref{sec:ops} measures how often that happened. \textbf{Stage 3} applies deterministic gates that can cap confidence or relabel a decision (e.g., an LLM HOLD with RSI$>$80 becomes WATCH~SELL). \textbf{Stage 4} sets ATR-based exits and writes each recommendation to a database with a UTC timestamp.

\section{The Forward Audit Protocol}
\label{sec:fap}
\subsection{Contamination-free signal set}
Every decision is logged at generation time, after the model's cutoff, so no future information can reach the model. Hourly rescans repeat calls, so we keep the first signal per (ticker, day, decision). Signal $i$ on ticker $s_i$ issued on day $t_i$ is entered at the close of $t_i$ (robustness: next open) for multi-day horizons, and at the close of the 60-minute bar containing the timestamp for the intraday horizon. Both entries occur strictly after the signal.

\subsection{Abnormal returns and event study}
With $P_{s,t}$ the adjusted close and $\beta_{s,t}$ a 60-day rolling beta estimated before $t$, the beta-adjusted cumulative abnormal return is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CAR}_i(h)=\frac{P_{s_i,t_i+h}}{P_{s_i,t_i}}-1-\beta_{s_i,t_i}\Bigl(\frac{M_{t_i+h}}{M_{t_i}}-1\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $M$ is SPY. We trace $\overline{\mathrm{CAR}}(h)$ for $h=0,\dots,10$~\cite{brown1985event}.

\subsection{Characteristic-matched placebo}
Comparing against SPY or random stocks conflates the LLM's choice with the pre-screen's tilt toward oversold, volatile names, which carries its own short-term reversal premium~\cite{jegadeesh1990evidence}. FAP instead builds, for each signal, a placebo of the $K$ stocks that look most similar to the chosen one on the same date but were \emph{not} chosen (Algorithm~\ref{alg:placebo})~\cite{daniel1997measuring}. The LLM's value-added is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i(h)=\mathrm{CAR}_i(h)-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_K(i)}\mathrm{CAR}_j(h).
\end{equation}
For multi-day horizons the features are daily RSI-14, 20-day momentum and ATR/price. For the intraday horizon they are the pre-screen's own features: hourly RSI-14, 5-bar momentum, hourly ATR/price and volume ratio, all measured at the last completed bar. We set $K{=}10$ and also report a random same-date control ($K{=}20$).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Characteristic-matched placebo}
\label{alg:placebo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE signal $(s_i,t_i)$; universe $\mathcal{U}$; LLM picks $\mathcal{S}_{t_i}$; features $x_{s,t}\in\mathbb{R}^d$
\STATE $\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{t_i}$ with complete data on $[t_i,t_i+h]$
\STATE $z_{s}\leftarrow(x_{s,t_i}-\mu_{\mathcal{C}})/\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}$ \COMMENT{cross-sectional z-score}
\STATE $\mathcal{N}_K(i)\leftarrow$ $K$ members of $\mathcal{C}$ minimizing $\lVert z_s-z_{s_i}\rVert_2$
\ENSURE $\Delta_i(h)$ via Eq.~(2)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Dependence-robust inference}
Signals on the same day share market shocks, and 10-day windows overlap. FAP resamples non-overlapping blocks of $L{=}10$ trading days (one day for intraday) with replacement~\cite{kunsch1989jackknife}, $B{=}5{,}000$ times, and reports percentile 95\% CIs and two-sided bootstrap $p$-values. Portfolio alphas use Newey--West standard errors with 10 lags~\cite{newey1987simple}.

\subsection{Specification--deployment consistency and exit decomposition}
Agent pipelines have hidden technical debt~\cite{sculley2015hidden}. FAP checks that four horizons agree: the prompt's horizon, the feature timeframe, the exit levels, and the holding period being evaluated. It then splits trade return into \emph{selection} (hold to horizon) and \emph{execution} (deployed exit minus hold). Execution is also replayed over a grid of stop/target multiples of \emph{daily} ATR. Intrabar ordering is unknown, so the stop is assumed to fill first, and gaps fill at the open. All trades pay 10~bps round trip.

\subsection{Calibration audit}
We treat stated confidence $c_i$ as a probability that the trade beats SPY and report the expected calibration error (ECE) over confidence bins~\cite{guo2017calibration}, the Brier score against a constant (climatological) forecast, and the block-bootstrapped Spearman correlation between $c_i$ and $\mathrm{CAR}_i(10)$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The production log contains \nRaw{} decisions from \dFirst{} to \dLast{}. After de-duplication, \nBuy{} BUY signals on \nTick{} tickers over \nDays{} trading days have complete 10-day forward data (outcomes through \dEvalEnd{}), with \sigPerDay{} signals per active day on average. We also evaluate \iN{} intraday BUY signals on \iDays{} days and \nWS{} WATCH~SELL signals (the LLM said HOLD on names with RSI$>$80), which serve as an overbought reference set. The mean stated confidence of BUYs is \meanConf{}. The scanner's universe is a fixed list of \nScannerUniv{} U.S. large- and mid-cap stocks, broader than the S\&P~500: of the \nLogTick{} tickers in the log, \nNonIndex{} (mid-cap and recently listed names; none is an ETF) were not index members on 2026-01-01, and they account for \nBuyNonIndexPct\% of the evaluated BUY signals. The placebo pool is the fixed list of \nIndexStart{} S\&P~500 constituents on 2026-01-01 (\nUniv{} with complete price data), reconstructed from Wikipedia's constituent list and a public index-change log. Non-members are evaluated as signals but never serve as placebo names. An earlier version of this analysis drew the pool from tickers that appeared in the log, which can peek ahead; Appendix~\ref{sec:regime} shows that this mattered. Prices are split- and dividend-adjusted daily and 60-minute bars from Yahoo Finance. Over the same window SPY returned \pct{\spyTotal}. Because of the API outage of Section~\ref{sec:ops}, most signals come from April onward (\perFullN{} of \nBuy{}), so the usable window is about five months, not nine.

\textbf{What is audited.} A BUY in the log is the LLM's decision after the Stage-3 gates: Stage~3 changed the stated confidence of \auStageChanged\% of BUYs, and HOLD decisions were almost never stored (67 of \nRaw{} rows), so the candidates the LLM saw and rejected are not available. FAP therefore measures the value added by \emph{the LLM plus its gates} against a placebo built from daily (multi-day) or pre-screen (intraday) features, which approximates but is not identical to the pre-screen's candidate set. Lineage checks out: \auTraced\% of the \auBuys{} database BUYs match a same-ticker LLM BUY in the production log, a median of \auOffset{} minutes earlier.

\textbf{Statistical power.} With bootstrap standard error $\mathrm{SE}$ of $\Delta$, the minimum detectable effect at 5\% two-sided significance and 80\% power is $\mathrm{MDE}=(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\mathrm{SE}\approx 2.80\,\mathrm{SE}$ (Table~\ref{tab:power}). A null result should be read as ``no edge larger than the MDE'', not as ``no edge''. This MDE is for the LLM-minus-placebo difference over all signals; comparisons of one selection rule with another (Table~\ref{tab:forward}) are noisier and use fewer independent blocks per window, hence their larger minimum detectable differences. Section~\ref{sec:valid} measures size and power directly instead of relying on this formula.

\textbf{What the null can and cannot rule out.} Only the intraday test is sharp: its interval excludes any edge above \pct{\mdeUpperIntra} per trade, below the 10~bps cost. The ten-day result therefore means ``no edge larger than about \mdeTen\%'', not ``no edge''.

\subsection{Does the audit itself work?}\label{sec:valid}
We validated the inference on the real price panel. \powSets{} fake LLMs each picked, on every date with real BUYs, the same number of stocks at random from the 50 lowest-RSI names (mimicking the pre-screen) and were compared with the identical matched placebo; by construction they have no skill. Two facts follow (Table~\ref{tab:powersim}). First, once the mean is centred, the procedures do not over-reject: the percentile block bootstrap used in this paper wrongly finds an edge in \powSizeBoot\% of fake LLMs (nominal 5\%), a cluster-robust $t$ test in \powSizeT\% and a sign-flip permutation test in \powSizePerm\%, so \powBlocks{} independent blocks suffice for size, and an injected edge of 1 pp is found \powPwrOne\% of the time (\powPwrHalf\% at 0.5 pp). This holds prices fixed and randomises only the picks, so it validates size and power against pick randomness, not against a different market path. Second, the placebo is biased for extreme picks: a fake LLM with no skill earns \powBias{} pp over its matched placebo (SD \powSD{} pp), so the right zero for an oversold picker is not zero, and the real LLM's \pct{\mBuyMinusMatch} lies below \powRealPct\% of the fake ones.

Table~\ref{tab:sens} shows that this is design-dependent and not a finding. Under every placebo design the LLM's alpha stays within noise of zero (from $\sensLo$ to $\sensHi$ pp, every interval spanning zero: \sensAllSpan), the bias of no-skill pickers ranges from $\sensBiasLo$ to $\sensBiasHi$ pp, and once the placebo also matches on 5-day return, distance below the 20-day high and beta, the LLM does worse than only \sensRichWorse\% of no-skill pickers. We therefore keep the conclusion at ``no detectable edge'' and do not claim negative selection skill.

The intraday result, which the abstract leads with, was validated the same way (\ipBlocks{} day-blocks; Tables~\ref{tab:powersim} and~\ref{tab:sens}). The bootstrap's false-positive rate is \ipSizeBoot\% (cluster-robust $t$: \ipSizeT\%, sign-flip: \ipSizePerm\%) at a nominal 5\%, an injected edge of 0.08 pp is found about four times in five, and, unlike the ten-day placebo, the intraday placebo is essentially unbiased: no-skill pickers earn \ipBias{} pp (SD \ipSD{} pp). Across the six placebo designs the LLM's intraday alpha ranges from $\ipLo$ to $\ipHi$ pp (every interval spanning zero: \ipAllSpan), no-skill pickers earn $\ipBiasLo$ to $\ipBiasHi$ pp, and under the paper's own design the LLM does worse than \ipWorseBase\% of them: suggestive, not significant. The abstract's claim that an intraday edge above $+0.035$\% is ruled out therefore stands, with the simulation's own resolution (an empirical minimum detectable effect of about \ipMDE{} pp) as its precision. The intraday standardisation uses all universe names valid at the bar rather than excluding the day's picks, a small approximation.

\subsection{Consistency over time and a pre-registered forward test}
Splitting the signal dates at 60\% (\fwdEarlyN{} signals in \fwdEarlyB{} independent ten-day blocks, then \fwdLateN{} in \fwdLateB{}) and evaluating six fixed hypotheses in each window (Table~\ref{tab:forward}) finds no detectable effect in either. The lowest-RSI rule's advantage over the universe is positive in both windows, yet its interval always spans zero; confidence gating and confidence ranking are null in both. Because signals cluster in time, the effective sample is the number of independent blocks (\fwdAllB{}), so the smallest detectable difference between rules is about \fwdMDE{} percentage points. The lowest-RSI hypothesis (H3) and H5 were suggested by development results, so these windows are exploratory for them; only forward data can confirm either. H6 is the primary test of selection skill: the ten-day alpha over the placebo minus the placebo's measured bias (Section~\ref{sec:valid}), with the bias estimated from seeded no-skill fake pickers on the same dates and the whole procedure fixed before any forward outcome existed. We therefore froze the six hypotheses, thresholds and a minimum sample (\fwdNeedS{} matured signals and \fwdNeedB{} independent blocks) in a committed pre-registration on 2026-09-25, before any outcome of the forward window existed. Below that sample the test reports ``insufficient data'' rather than findings. \fwdStatusText{} Four further exploratory analyses, a purged walk-forward meta-model, an input ablation, a portfolio simulation and a regime split, are in Appendices~\ref{app:meta}--\ref{sec:regime}; none changes the conclusion.

\subsection{Specification--deployment mismatch: a counterfactual exit analysis}
The project was designed for 5--10-day swing trades, and the codebase contains a swing prompt that asks for stops of 2--3$\times$ \emph{daily} ATR. The consistency audit found that the production scanner instead called the \emph{intraday} prompt on 60-minute bars, with stops at 1.5$\times$ \emph{hourly} ATR, and closed every position the same day: of \prodN{} BUYs with a recorded exit, \prodEod\% were closed at the end of day and the other \prodStopN{} by the intraday stop. No position was ever held for ten days with these levels, so the analysis below is a \emph{counterfactual}: it asks what would have happened had the deployed stop and target been attached to the ten-day hold the project was designed for. Measured against daily ATR, that stop is only \medStopATR{}$\times$ (median \medStopPct\% below entry). Table~\ref{tab:exit} shows the replay: \depStopRate\% of positions would have been stopped out, the win rate would have fallen from \holdWin\% to \depWin\%, and mean net return from \pct{\holdNet} to \pct{\depNet}, a difference of \pct{\depGap} per trade (CI \pct{\depGapCI}). Fig.~\ref{fig:grid} shows that across the grid, returns rise steadily as stops widen, and no stop or target beats simply holding (\pct{\gridBest}); the tightest setting earns \pct{\gridHalfNoTgt}. Because the replay applies an intraday rule to a horizon it was not built for, it quantifies a configuration error, not a realised loss. Its realised counterpart is the intraday analysis, where the deployed levels stopped \iStop\% of trades (\prodStopN{} of \prodN{} in production) and changed mean return by only \pct{\iDepGap} (CI \pct{\iDepGapCI}).

\subsection{Calibration of the recorded confidence}
\textbf{Native intraday horizon.} At the horizon the prompt was written for, stated confidence averaged \iConf{} while \iHit{}\% of BUYs beat SPY the same day, a calibration gap of \iEce{}. \textbf{Ten-day horizon.} Graded against a question the scores were not written for, confidence was concentrated at \calConf{} while only \calWin\% of BUYs beat SPY over ten days (Table~\ref{tab:calib}, Fig.~\ref{fig:calib}). The ECE is \ece{} and the Brier score is \brier{}, worse than the \brierBase{} of a constant forecast. The rank correlation with CAR is $\rho=\rhoConf$ (CI $\rhoConfCI$), so confidence did not even order trades correctly. Using it to gate trades, as Stage~3 does, is therefore unfounded. Because Stage~3 altered the confidence of \auStageChanged\% of BUYs, the audited value is the pipeline's, not the model's alone, so the resemblance to evidence that LLMs verbalize over-confidence~\cite{xiong2024uncertainty} is suggestive only; raw model confidence is logged from September 2026 on.

\subsection{Operational audit of the production log}
\label{sec:ops}
The production log covers \opScans{} hourly scans (median duration \opScanMedMin{} minutes, about \opSelMed{} candidates per scan) and contains \opDecisions{} decisions. Only \opReal{} (\opRealPct\%) came from the model. \section{Discussion}
\textbf{Lessons for AI engineering.} (1) \emph{Attribute before you celebrate.} The agent's portfolio (Appendix~\ref{app:port}) beat SPY on Sharpe (\pLlmSh{} vs.\ \pSpySh{}) and its placebo (\pMatchSh{}), yet neither its alpha ($t{=}\pLlmAlphaT$) nor its long--short spread over the placebo ($t{=}\pLSAlphaT$) is distinguishable from zero, and the placebo's own Sharpe moved from \prevMatchSh{} to \pMatchSh{} when the pool was fixed. P\&L is a property of the whole pipeline, and the LLM's contribution must be measured against a counterfactual that removes only the LLM's choice. (2) \emph{Specification drift is a first-class failure mode.} A single wrong flag sent the intraday prompt into production for a swing strategy. Every component then behaved ``correctly'' in isolation while a counterfactual replay shows it would have lost \pct{\depGap} per trade on the intended horizon. Consistency checks between prompt horizon, feature timeframe, exit rules and evaluation horizon belong in the test suite. (3) \emph{Do not gate on the recorded confidence} unless it has been calibrated on forward data, and log the model's raw confidence separately from the gated one. (4) \emph{Log the failure path.} Seven in ten decisions came from a silent exception fallback that emitted a plausible-looking HOLD; fallbacks need their own label, counter and alert.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} The sample spans nine months, \nDays{} signal days and a single model (GPT-4o-mini), so power is limited (Table~\ref{tab:power}); a 2024-vintage model says nothing about frontier models. The model was called by the alias gpt-4o-mini and the snapshot served during the study was not logged (the system now logs it; on 2026-09-25 the alias resolved to gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18), so a silent snapshot change cannot be excluded. Decisions come from a stochastic process (temperature 0.3): in a pilot replay of 38 candidates two identical runs disagreed on 6.6\% of decisions, and every result here is one realisation of it. The matched placebo is biased for oversold picks (Section~\ref{sec:valid}), and the pre-registration, though committed to the project repository, carries only the author's own timestamps. The February gap is not random downtime but the quota outage of Table~\ref{tab:ops}, and signals from that period rest on the small share of candidates that reached the model. The placebo pool is a fixed list of index constituents reconstructed from two public web sources that we did not independently audit, and it excludes the \nNonIndex{} non-index tickers the scanner also traded, so matching is among index names only. After the study period the scan list was aligned to the current index (2026-09-25), so forward results come from a slightly different set of stocks; that change is logged with the pre-registration. Stage-3 gates edit the LLM's output, HOLDs were not stored, and the multi-day placebo uses daily rather than pre-screen features, so the audit measures the LLM plus its gates against an approximation of its candidate set. The ten-day exit analysis is a counterfactual, since no position was held ten days. We assume 10~bps costs, no market impact and error-free Yahoo Finance bars; token counts and per-decision API cost were not logged. FAP does not prove that LLM agents cannot add value, only that this agent did not do so measurably.

\section{Conclusion}
We ran a multi-agent LLM trading system in production after the model's knowledge cutoff and audited its \nRaw{} logged decisions with the Forward Audit Protocol. The LLM's stock selection added no statistically detectable return over characteristic-matched alternatives at any horizon (minimum detectable effects in Table~\ref{tab:power}). A counterfactual replay shows that the deployed intraday stop levels would have turned the captured market drift into a flat result, the pipeline's recorded confidence was uninformative, and \opFallbackPct\% of the logged decisions were quota-exhaustion fallbacks. FAP (contamination-free logging, matched placebos, block-bootstrap inference with stated power, consistency, calibration and operational audits) is cheap to adopt, but it has been validated on one system only and its placebo needs a bias correction for extreme picks. We offer it as a starting point for auditing LLM agents, not as a settled standard. A purged walk-forward meta-model found no predictor of which calls work, and an input ablation showed that only the technical block moves decisions beyond the model's own randomness. A pre-registered forward test (six fixed hypotheses; minimum sample of \fwdNeedS{} signals in \fwdNeedB{} independent blocks) is running and will be reported once it matures. Future work will compare frontier models under the same protocol and re-test regime effects out of sample.

\section{Can anything predict which calls work?}\label{app:meta}
We tried to learn which BUYs beat their matched placebo, using purged walk-forward validation (expanding window, 10-day purge and 5-day embargo; \metaOof{} out-of-fold signals in \metaBlocks{} independent blocks) with two model families fixed in advance. Feature set A is price context only (RSI, momentum, distance below the 20-day high, ATR, beta, VIX, market trend, days to earnings); set B adds what the LLM said (stated confidence and trend label). Fundamentals, news and market fields could not be used, because the historical log recorded them for only about 10 of 1,493 BUYs. No model predicts the outcome out of sample (Table~\ref{tab:meta}): AUC is at or below chance, every selective-alpha interval spans zero, and the LLM's outputs add nothing measurable in either model family. The logistic models score below chance, meaning relations learned in earlier months reversed later. Of \metaTests{} tests, only the lowest-RSI third of BUYs underperforming survives a Holm correction, and only marginally (adjusted $p{=}\metaLowRsiHolm$); it was not hypothesised in advance, and it is not stable across windows ($-0.10$\% early, $-0.78$\% late, Table~\ref{tab:forward}), so we pre-registered it as H5 for the forward test rather than reporting it as a finding. As checks on the pipeline, its out-of-fold AUC stays near 0.5 on shuffled noise and a planted signal is recovered (AUC above 0.7), so the null is not an artifact of the procedure. The minimum detectable selective alpha is about \metaMDE{} percentage points. The analysis is exploratory: the target is a noisy placebo-relative alpha, the model families, the 30\% cut and the features were chosen by the analyst, and Holm covers only the tests shown. A below-chance AUC for the logistic models more likely reflects instability across periods than a stable, reversed relation.

\section{Which inputs move the decisions?}\label{sec:abl}
We replayed the exact logged inputs of \ablN{} candidates from one scan through the production prompt, three times unchanged and once with each input group replaced by neutral text (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). Two identical runs disagree on $100-\ablNoise\%$ of decisions, so the model's own randomness sets the bar. Removing the technical block changes decisions beyond it, after which the model buys far more often. This survives a second, differently worded replacement (58\% agreement, BUY rate 47\%), so it is not an artifact of the wording, although its size depends on it. News, fundamentals and market context each stay within noise at this sample size under every wording tried: removing news lowered the BUY rate (3\% and 8\% under the two wordings, control \ablBuyCtl\%) and removing fundamentals raised it, but these directions rest on a few flipped decisions and are not established. The retrieval-augmented context is not an ablation group: it is computed but never inserted into the prompt, so it cannot affect any decision. This is a one-scan pilot and a replacement string is never fully information-free. A weekly job now replays a sample and will report whether removing an input changes which BUYs earn returns once ten-day outcomes mature.
